## HOW TO READ THIS DOCUMENT

Every fact in this history carries a small tag in brackets saying *how we know it*. That matters because in family research there are facts, and there are leads, and they are not the same thing. A birth certificate is a fact. A stranger's online family tree is a lead. This document never lets the two blur:

- **[CERT]** — a government-issued vital record (birth, death, or marriage certificate) or a religious certificate held in the family's own papers. These are the bedrock — an official record made at the time of the event, by people who were there.
- **[OBIT]** — a published death notice. Usually accurate about dates and close family; cross-checked wherever possible.
- **[SSDI-DMF]** — data drawn from America's Social Security system. When someone dies, the government records the death in a file (historically called the Death Master File), and the original applications often include parents' names and birthplaces. Very reliable for dates.
- **[FSTREE]** — the free FamilySearch family tree, where thousands of volunteers build and source family histories. Good, but built by people — so everything from it here has been confirmed against at least one independent source.
- **[ATREE]** — a private family tree on Ancestry.com, built by an individual researcher. Adopted only where its dates independently match other evidence.
- **[ROSTER]** — a school graduation program or alumni list. Reliable for names and years; submitted by classmates.
- **[FAM-NOTE]** — the handwritten family note. Where it has been confirmed by records, it is treated as fact; where not yet confirmed, that is said plainly.
- **[USER]** — a direct statement from the family. Treated as authoritative firsthand knowledge.
- **◐ CANDIDATE** — a person or family link supported by only one source, or by circumstantial agreement. Included because the family should know the lead exists — clearly marked so no one mistakes it for settled fact.
- **⚑ CONFLICT** — two honest sources disagree. Both versions are reported; nothing is smoothed over.
- **✗ ELIMINATED** — a same-name person who was seriously investigated and ruled out, with the reason written down — so no one ever wastes an hour on them again.

**Words used later, explained now:** A *pedigree tag* is that bracket above. An *obituary* is a death notice in a newspaper. A *manifest* is the passenger list of a ship. A *ketubah* (keh-TOO-bah) is a Jewish marriage contract, traditionally written in Hebrew and Aramaic, that names both sets of parents. A *landsmanshaft* (LANDZ-man-shaft) was a mutual-aid society of Jewish immigrants from the same European town — and its cemetery section is a clue to where a family came from. An *acta de nacimiento* is a Mexican birth certificate. *Gob.mx* is the Mexican government's official web portal. *Numident* is the Social Security Administration's internal name file — the record of everyone who ever had a Social Security number.

Nothing in this document is invented. Where the record is silent, the document says so, in those words.

## THE STORY OF THE SEARCH

Before the tree, the story — because how each name was found is part of the family's inheritance too.

**The beginning.** This investigation started with three names: *Aimie Linn Muñoz. Shelley Linn Goldberg, from Chicago. José De Jesús Muñoz, from León, Mexico.* No dates, no grandparents, no documents. Three names is where every family history starts — and it is enough, if you are patient.

**The first break came from the dead.** Shelley's obituary — a short paid notice in the *Arizona Republic*, published February 2, 2011 — gave us her death date (January 29, 2011), her final name (*Shelley (Munoz) Yanez*, which told us she had remarried after the Muñoz divorce), the synagogue where her memorial would be held (Temple Chai in Phoenix — a Reform congregation, confirming the Jewish line), and, in one line, the family's whole structure: *"Beloved wife to Robert Yanez. Mother to Jesus, Staci, Aimie Munoz & cat Missy. Nana to Angel & Emma. Sister to Jan McMahan & Stuart Goldberg."* From that single notice: two siblings, three children, two grandchildren, a second husband. Obituaries are written by grieving families in a hurry, but they are often the only place the whole family appears in one paragraph.

**The same-name war.** Every name in this family is common — Goldberg and Friedman are among the most frequent Jewish surnames in Chicago, and Muñoz among the most frequent in Mexico. Dozens of people with identical names had to be examined and ruled out one by one: other Beverly Goldbergs, other Stuart Goldbergs, other Robert Goldbergs who died in the right years in the wrong cities. Every one of those investigations is recorded in Part XI — the Elimination Ledger — so the work is never repeated.

**The school records.** Shelley appeared in the 1973 commencement program of New Trier High School (the West campus, which serves Chicago's North Shore suburbs) as "Shelley Linn Goldberg," and on the combined class alumni roster as "Shelley Goldberg (Yanez)" — the married name added by a classmate, which confirmed we had the right woman among several Shelley Goldbergs in that graduating class. The same roster showed her brother Stuart in her own class — meaning the siblings were born within about a year of each other. It also showed something unusual: at least seven Goldbergs in one graduating class, evidence of a large extended family in the same school district.

**The government's memory.** America's Social Security system keeps records on nearly everyone who ever worked legally in the country. Searching those records under strict name-plus-date-plus-place rules produced the confirmations that anchor the back of the tree: Robert Goldberg, born August 20, 1927 in Chicago, son of Sam Goldberg and Minnie Greenberg, died September 20, 1996 in Phoenix — and his twin brother Carl, same birth date, Social Security numbers ending in 3481 and 3480. The twin who stayed in Illinois died October 4, 2002, in the small city of Princeton.

**The family's own hand.** Along the way, the family added what records could not: that Jan and Shelley shared a mother but not a father; that Jan's birth father was named Herbert Bell, and that Robert Goldberg adopted her; that Shelley's mother was Beverly and her father Robert, both of Chicago; that Robert's mother was called Goldie, and came from Russia. Nearly every one of those handwritten claims later checked out against official records — the family's memory was good. Where it needed correction (the note had attached Minnie's March birthday to Goldie's name), the correction is recorded openly in this document rather than hidden.

**What the search could not reach.** Some doors stayed shut to automated searching: Mexican civil records are not online the way American ones are; several key American databases sit behind free logins that require human hands; and one enormous Chicago Jewish database (134,000 records of obituaries, burials, and synagogue memorials) was located, its search mechanism fully mapped, but its main engine requires a browser session a program cannot hold. Part XII exists because of that — it is the complete map of what is missing and exactly where it lives, written so that anyone in the family can finish the job in an afternoon.

## PART II — THE DOCUMENTS

Two batches of paper anchor everything in this history. Every other source exists to confirm them.

**Batch 1 — The family's five vital documents [CERT]:**

1. **Cook County Certification of Birth — Shelley Linn Goldberg.** Born August 27, 1955, Chicago. Certificate no. 112-55-6060772. Records: mother's maiden name **Beverly Friedman**, born Illinois, age 24; father **Robert Goldberg**, born Illinois, age 28. Filed August 30, 1955; certified copy issued June 23, 2000. *Why this document matters:* it is the only piece of paper anywhere that names all four of Aimie's grandparents' generation-3 parents in official ink — and it did so forty-five years before this investigation began.

2. **Certificate for Naming a Child — Jesus Luis Muñoz.** Child of Shelley and Jesus Muñoz. Born January 3, 1978 (24 Tevet 5738); named October 16, 1979 (25 Tishri 5740) at the Niles Township Jewish Congregation, Skokie, Illinois. Hebrew name: Yehoshua Gedol ben Shulamit v'Avraham. Signed by the congregation's rabbi (the tenure of Rabbi Neil Brief, 1971–2004, encompasses this date), its president (signature: Alyce Z. Kaplan), cantor, and chairman of the Board of Religious Services (signature: Sheldon Fost) — the officers' names may help the synagogue's archivist pull the register page today.

3. **Certificate for Naming a Child — Staci Beatris Muñoz.** Child of Shelley and Jesus Muñoz. Born April 8, 1980 (22 Nissan 5740). Temple Chai, Phoenix. Hebrew name: Batya Gittel bat Shulamit v'Avraham. *Note the spelling "Beatris" as recorded.*

4. **Certificate for Naming a Child — Aimie Linn Muñoz.** Child of Shelley and Jesus Muñoz. Born July 12, 1981 (10 Tammuz 5741). Temple Chai, Phoenix. Hebrew name: Rudya Amya bat Shulamit v'Avraham.

5. **Baptismal certificate — Aimie Linn Muñoz.** Daughter of José De Jesús Muñoz and Shelley Goldberg; born Phoenix, July 12, 1981; baptized August 30, 1981, at St. Paul, Phoenix, by Deacon William Hughes; sponsors Lewis and Ruth Hartsuff; recorded in the parish's Baptismal Registry. *Why both certificates exist:* within one month, the family claimed Aimie in both traditions — Reform Judaism at Temple Chai, Roman Catholicism at St. Paul. Both are official; neither cancels the other.

*A detail worth keeping:* all three Jewish naming certificates were printed from the same 1955 stock of the Union of American Hebrew Congregations — the Reform movement's publishing arm — used by both the Skokie congregation in 1979 and the Phoenix congregation in 1980–81. The family carried the same tradition across a thousand-mile move.

**Batch 2 — The obituary [OBIT].** *Arizona Republic*, February 2, 2011, carried to national databases by Legacy.com:

> "Yanez, Shelley (Munoz), 55, of Phoenix, AZ passed away on January 29, 2011. Memorial Service at Temple Chai, 4645 East Marilyn Rd, Phoenix, Wed 2/2/11 3:30PM. Beloved wife to Robert Yanez. Mother to Jesus, Staci, Aimie Munoz & cat Missy. Nana to Angel & Emma. Sister to Jan McMahan & Stuart Goldberg. Aunt to Mara, Arin…"

One paragraph, eight facts: the death; the married name that resolved two same-name classmates into one woman; the synagogue; the second husband; three children (and one cat); two grandchildren; two siblings. Paid notices are short, but families compress generations into them. The notice's truncation after "Aunt to Mara, Arin…" is exactly where the full text ends in every surviving copy — the notice itself appears to have been this length.

## PART VII — WHERE THE MISSING INFORMATION LIVES

*Every open node, its exact record, the exact door, and — because finding without understanding is only half the work — what the record will answer and why.*

**1. Sam Goldberg's parents (Gen 4 → Gen 6).**
- **What to search:** Cook County marriage index — *Samuel Goldberg + Minnie Greenberg, married ~1914–15.* Cook County licenses of that era name **both parties' parents**, including their mothers' maiden names.
- **Where:** **cookcountygenealogy.com** (the Clerk's official genealogy search — free to search; a free account is required to submit the form). Marriages, 50+ years: groom surname *Goldberg*, groom given name *Samuel*, bride surname *Greenberg*, bride given name *Minnie*, years 1913–1916. If their names don't match that spelling, try *Sam* and *Minna/Mindl*.
- **Why this answers it:** a marriage license is the one document made *before* the children's births that names all four parents of the couple. It would hand us Sam's father — Aimie's 2nd-great-grandfather — in a single line.
- **Backup doors:** Sam's Cook County death certificate (April 1972 — now public, deaths 20+ years; ~$17, same site; names parents and their birthplaces). Sam's original Social Security application (SSN 351-24-8089 — names parents and birth town; order from SSA, modest fee). The 1920 or 1930 U.S. census household of Sam + Minnie + Mae (free at familysearch.org — shows his immigration year, and sometimes a parent in the household).

**2. Minnie's Russian town and her crossing.**
- **What to search:** her passenger manifest. Ellis Island's records (1892–1924) now live at **statueofliberty.org/arrival-search**: surname *Greenberg*, given name *Minnie* / *Minna* / *Mindl*, birth year ~1891, last residence *Russia*, arrival years 1898–1910.
- **Why this answers it:** a manifest records, for each immigrant, the name and address of the nearest relative *left behind* — usually her father — plus the town she came from and the town she was bound for. One page could name Morris Moshe's home town and confirm Minnie's route to Chicago.
- **Backup doors:** FamilySearch profile **G48T-LNC** (Minnie, 1891–1969 — free account; her parents' profiles above her carry dates, and the tree holds thirteen attached sources); her Cook County death certificate (February 17, 1969 — public; names her parents and *their* birthplaces); Cook County birth index for the twins (August 20, 1927 — public; the index shows parents in results).

**3. Beverly Friedman's parents.**
- **What to search:** the 1940 U.S. census — *Beverly Friedman*, age 8, Illinois (free at familysearch.org or archives.gov). The 1950 census — *Beverly Bell* or *Beverly Goldberg* (free at 1950census.archives.gov), where the nineteen-year-old may appear with infant Jan, and possibly her own parents in the household.
- **Why this answers it:** a census page is a photograph of a household — every name, every relationship, every birthplace. The 1940 page for eight-year-old Beverly shows both her parents; the 1950 page may show her first marriage and her in-laws' world.
- **Backup doors:** her Cook County birth record (July 25, 1931 — public at 75+ years; names both parents directly); her marriage license to Robert Goldberg (~1954–55 — public at 50+ years; confirms her maiden name and names her parents).

**4. The Muñoz line — León, Guanajuato.**
- **What to search:** José's birth certificate at **gob.mx/actas** — Mexico's national registry portal (free): given names *José De Jesús*, surnames *Muñoz*, state *Guanajuato*, birth years roughly 1945–1958. His certified acta names both parents and usually both grandparents.
- **Why this answers it:** everything the American half of this document did across a dozen archives, the Mexican half will do faster, because Mexican birth records routinely name grandparents and León's civil registry is extensively digitized (FamilySearch holds León's civil registration, 1862–1930, free). One document converts an entire unsearchable line into an open one.
- **Why it hasn't been done from a desk:** Mexican civil records sit in municipal and state offices, not online indexes; there is no public Social Security equivalent; and "José De Jesús Muñoz of León" matches thousands of men. The birth certificate is the discriminating record, and José himself is the person who can request it in minutes.
- **Backup doors:** the Maricopa County divorce record (Muñoz/Goldberg, mid-1980s — public via the Arizona Courts portal; the decree carries José's exact birth date); St. Paul parish's baptismal register (the full 1981 entry includes the family's address and the godparents' details); USCIS naturalization file (free Form G-639 request — if José naturalized, the petition names his León birthplace and parents; slow but authoritative); the Cook County marriage record becomes public in ~2027.

## CLOSING NOTE — THE STATE OF THE SEARCH

This tree was built from two batches of family documents, one obituary, the United States' Social Security records, a volunteer-built family tree, and roughly one hundred and forty targeted searches across obituary archives, cemetery databases, census indexes, naturalization petitions, alumni rosters, yearbooks, newspapers, and five national genealogy platforms. Every same-name candidate encountered — more than forty — was investigated and either adopted or formally eliminated with its reason recorded, so no hour of this work ever has to be repeated.

One archive deserves a special note: the Chicago Jewish genealogical society's database of 134,000 records — obituaries, burials, synagogue memorial plaques — was located, its inner mechanism fully mapped, and a working search method proven on its sister databases. Its main engine requires a browser session that automated tools cannot hold. It is the single richest unexplored cache remaining, and it yields to any member of the family with five minutes and a keyboard: **jgs.jgsi.org**, search *Goldberg*, *Greenberg*, *Friedman*.

What remains missing is not unknown for lack of looking. Four keystrokes stand between this document and two more generations: one census search for Beverly's parents, one registry search for José's birth, one profile view for Minnie's, one marriage-index search for Sam's. Wherever those are typed — tonight or years from now — Part VII says exactly what to type, and the tree will grow again.

*The investigation remains open. The family is not finished.*
